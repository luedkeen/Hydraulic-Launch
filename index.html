Sizing a hydraulic launch, from accumulator to catch car

How do you size the pumps, accumulators, and supply lines for a hydraulic roller-coaster launch so a train reaches its target speed in the required time — without losing too much pressure to friction along the way? This site is the project: an interactive sizing demo, the full calculation, and the documentation of how it was built with AI.

In plain English

Some roller coasters don't roll down a big hill to get going. Instead, a machine yanks the train forward — really fast — like a slingshot. This project answers a simple question: how big does that slingshot machine need to be? How strong a push, how big the pipes, and how big the pump — so the train hits 78 mph in under 3 seconds without wasting energy along the way. Every technical explanation on this page now has a plain-English version right next to it, like this one. The math stays — your professor still needs it — but you don't need an engineering degree to follow the story.

The core tradeoff: pressure loss vs. pipe diameter

At the design flow rate, friction loss collapses as diameter grows — roughly with 1/D⁵ — but a bigger line holds more oil, costs more, and is heavier. The chart and table update with your slider settings; the highlighted row is your current diameter.

In plain English

Bigger pipe = less waste, but more money. A wide pipe lets the oil glide through easily, so almost no energy is wasted. A narrow pipe forces the oil to squeeze and rub, wasting energy. But wide pipes cost more, weigh more, and hold more oil. So the job is picking the “just right” size — not too wasteful, not needlessly huge. That's why this project lands on the middle size, 0.15 m (about 6 inches across).

Section 2 · The system

Store slowly, spend fast

A hydraulic launch (Intamin's Accelerator Coaster design, used on Kingda Ka and Top Thrill Dragster) separates making power from delivering it. Modest pumps spend ~45 seconds charging accumulators; the launch then spends that stored energy in under 3 seconds. That is why the peak power can be ~20× the pump power — the central Transport 1 idea of this project.

In plain English

Here's the trick, and it's the whole idea of the project: save up slowly, spend it all at once. A normal pump isn't strong enough to launch a coaster by itself — that would take a gigantic, expensive motor. So instead, small pumps spend about 45 seconds slowly squeezing oil into storage tanks, like winding up a toy or inflating a balloon. Then — whoosh — the tanks release everything in under 3 seconds. That burst is about 20 times stronger than the pumps alone. It's the same reason you charge your phone for an hour and then it can flash its camera light in a split second: storing energy lets you spend it faster than you made it. Read the 7 boxes below left to right — that's the journey the oil takes, from quiet tank to flying train.

1 · ReservoirHydraulic oil tank at atmospheric pressure
2 · Pumps~500 hp class units push oil toward the accumulators over ~45 s
3 · AccumulatorsOil compresses nitrogen across a piston — a fluid spring storing ~6.6 MJ
4 · Launch valvesOpen in a fraction of a second; nitrogen expands and drives the oil out
5 · Hydraulic motorsConvert ΔP·Q into shaft torque at up to ~175 L/s
6 · Winch & cableMotors spin a drum; the cable pulls the catch car down the track
7 · Catch car & trainCatch car engages the train and accelerates it to target speed
In plain English — the 7 steps, simply

1. Oil sits in a tank. 2. Pumps slowly squeeze it into storage tanks. 3. The storage tanks hold it under high pressure, like a coiled spring. 4. Valves snap open. 5. The rushing oil spins motors. 6. The motors wind a cable, like a giant fishing reel. 7. The cable yanks a small cart — hooked to the train — down the track, and off you go.

Reference case: Kingda Ka launched 0 → 128 mph (57.2 m/s) in 3.5 s (≈1.67 g) with a hydraulic launch motor rated at 20,800 peak hp (15.5 MW), accumulator pressures reported up to ~300 bar, and recharge in ~45 s with all pumps running. Intamin does not publish line diameters or accumulator volumes, so this project sizes its own self-consistent design case (Section 3) and uses Kingda Ka only as an order-of-magnitude validation (Section 4). In plain English: the company that built Kingda Ka never published its pipe or tank sizes, so instead of guessing them, this project designs its own slightly smaller launch from scratch — and only uses Kingda Ka as a reality check in Section 4.

Theory 1 · The physical picture

A hydraulic launch is an energy-timing problem before it is a plumbing problem. The train needs 4.59 MJ of kinetic energy delivered in 2.8 s, and — because thrust is roughly constant while speed keeps rising — the instantaneous power at the end of the launch (3.46 MW mechanical) is about twice the average (1.64 MW). No reasonably sized pump delivers that peak directly, so the system separates making power from spending it: pumps charge nitrogen accumulators slowly, and the compressed gas — a fluid spring — spends the stored energy in one burst through the motors and winch. Two loss mechanisms then decide whether the design works. First, every component keeps a cut (the efficiencies η). Second, the oil must travel from accumulator to motor through a real pipe, and the pressure it loses to wall friction and fittings grows brutally as the pipe narrows — roughly with 1/D⁵ — which is why line sizing, a pure Transport 1 question, sits at the centre of the design. The “runaway” analogue in this system is not thermal but hydraulic: undersize the line a little and the friction loss does not grow a little, it explodes, and the launch falls short of speed no matter how big the accumulators are.

In plain English

The train needs a lot of energy, all at once, and the hardest moment is the very end of the launch. So the machine cheats on timing: charge slowly, spend fast. Then two things can ruin it — parts that waste a cut of the energy, and a pipe that's too skinny. The skinny-pipe problem is sneaky: shrink the pipe a bit and the waste doesn't grow a bit, it balloons. Picking the pipe is therefore the heart of this project.

Theory 2 · The model: control volumes, balances, and assumptions

The model uses two control volumes, and each governing equation in Steps 1–5 is a balance on one of them. The train is treated as a control mass: the linear momentum balance ΣF = m·a on it gives the thrust, and the mechanical-energy balance on it gives the kinetic energy and power the fluid system must supply (Step 1). The supply line is a fixed control volume between accumulator and motor: the mass balance on it is continuity, v = Q/A (Step 3), and the mechanical-energy balance on it is what the pressure is spent on — useful pressure drop across the motor (Step 2) minus the Darcy–Weisbach friction loss and the minor losses in fittings (Step 3). The accumulator is a third, deformable control volume: an energy balance over one launch sets the stored energy (Step 4), and the nitrogen inside it follows a polytropic path, P·Vn = const, as it expands. Step 5 is the Step 4 energy balance run in reverse, slowly, through the pump. The model rests on four assumptions, stated up front:

Modeling assumptions

  • Constant acceleration as a first approximation; in reality thrust is highest at the start and falls as accumulator pressure drops.
  • Incompressible oil; quasi-steady snapshots at start / middle / end of launch.
  • Efficiencies (motor 0.85, overall 0.70, pump 0.80) taken as representative values, not measured curves.
  • Drag allowance lumped as a constant 5 kN rather than a v² model.

Theory 5 below puts a price on the biggest of these simplifications — the constant-thrust picture, the friction-only line, and the accumulator shortcut — so their cost is on the record rather than buried.

Step 1 — Train dynamics (momentum balance on the train)

Derivation. Take the train as the control mass. The linear momentum balance ΣF = m·a, with a taken constant, integrates directly to v = a·t and s = ½·a·t². The cable thrust must supply m·a plus the lumped drag allowance. Force × velocity then gives the instantaneous mechanical power — which peaks at top speed, because the force is constant while v is still rising — and ½·m·v² is the energy every later step has to deliver.

a = v / t = 35 / 2.8 = 12.5 m/s² (1.27 g)
s = ½·a·t² = 49 m
F = m·a + Fdrag = 7,500 × 12.5 + 5,000 ≈ 98.8 kN → design thrust ≈ 100 kN
KE = ½·m·v² = 4.59 MJ
Ppeak,mech = F·v = 3.46 MW (constant thrust: power peaks at top speed) · Pavg = KE / t = 1.64 MW

In plain English — Step 1

How hard do we need to push? The train weighs about as much as five cars (7,500 kg) and has to reach highway speed (78 mph) in 2.8 seconds. That takes a push of roughly 100,000 newtons — imagine about 10 tonnes of weight shoving it forward. Riders feel it as being pressed back into their seats at about 1.3 times normal gravity. The train needs about 49 meters of track to do it — roughly half a football field — and by the end it's carrying 4.59 million joules of moving energy. For scale, that's about the energy in a cup of gasoline, all delivered in under 3 seconds.

Step 2 — Motor flow rate

Derivation. Hydraulic power is pressure × flow by definition: P = ΔP·Q is (force ÷ area) × (volume ÷ time). The motor converts that power to shaft power with efficiency ηmotor, so the oil must carry Ppeak,mech ÷ ηmotor. Dividing by the pressure available at the sizing moment (Theory 7) gives the flow the line, valves, and motor must pass.

Phyd = ΔP · Q  →  Phyd,peak = Ppeak,mech / ηmotor = 3.46 / 0.85 ≈ 4.07 MW
Qpeak = Phyd,peak / Pworking ≈ 4.07 MW / 22 MPa ≈ 0.185 m³/s ≈ 185 L/s
Symbols in Step 2 — where they first appear
  • Phyd — hydraulic power carried by the oil (W; shown in MW). The rule is Phyd = ΔP · Q: pressure times flow.
  • ΔP — pressure difference driving the oil through the motor (Pa, pascals; shown in MPa). Δ (delta) means “difference across.”
  • Q — flow rate, how much oil passes per second (m³/s; also shown in L/s).
  • Phyd,peak — peak hydraulic power, the Step 1 mechanical peak grossed up for motor losses (W; shown in MW). Here ≈ 4.07 MW.
  • ηmotor — motor efficiency (η is Greek eta), the share of hydraulic power that becomes mechanical power (no units, a fraction 0–1). Here ηmotor = 0.85, i.e. 85% gets through.
  • Qpeak — the largest flow rate in the launch, at the sizing moment near the end (m³/s; shown in L/s). Here ≈ 0.185 m³/s ≈ 185 L/s.
  • Pworking — working pressure, the accumulator pressure available near the end of the launch (Pa; shown in MPa). Here ≈ 22 MPa.

Working pressure is taken near the end of the launch, when accumulator pressure has fallen toward its minimum — that is when the same power demands the most flow, so it is the conservative sizing point.

In plain English — Step 2

How much oil has to move? Power from oil works like this: push hard × flow fast = power. Near the end of the launch, the storage tank's pressure has dropped (it's running low, like a balloon going soft), so to keep the same push, the oil has to flow faster to make up for it. At its hungriest moment, the motor needs about 185 liters of oil every second — that's like emptying two full bathtubs per second through the pipe. We size everything for that worst moment, so the launch never runs out of breath.

Step 3 — Line sizing: continuity, Reynolds, Darcy–Weisbach

Derivation. Apply the two balances to the line as a fixed control volume. The mass balance, for incompressible oil at steady flow, is continuity: v = Q/A. The mechanical-energy balance between accumulator and motor then spends the available pressure three ways — across the motor (useful, Step 2), on wall friction, and on fittings. Friction is Darcy–Weisbach, ΔPf = f·(L/D)·(ρv²/2), with the friction factor f taken from the Haaland approximation to the Moody chart at the flow's Reynolds number Re = vD/ν and relative roughness ε/D. Each fitting costs K dynamic heads, ΔPminor = ΣK·(ρv²/2).

v = Q / A · Re = v·D / ν · 1/√f = −1.8·log₁₀[ (ε/D / 3.7)1.11 + 6.9/Re ] (Haaland)
ΔPf = f · (L/D) · (ρv²/2) · ΔPminor = ΣK · (ρv²/2), ΣK = 5
Symbols in Step 3 — where they first appear
  • v — here, the speed of the oil inside the pipe (not the train) (m/s). Same letter, new meaning — the equation v = Q ÷ A is about the pipe.
  • A — cross-sectional area of the pipe opening, A = π·D²/4 (m²). (π is the circle constant, ≈ 3.1416, with no units.)
  • Re — Reynolds number, a unit-free score for smooth (laminar) vs. swirly (turbulent) flow (no units, dimensionless). Tens of thousands here = turbulent.
  • D — inside diameter of the supply pipe (m). Compared at 0.10, 0.15 and 0.20 m.
  • ν — kinematic viscosity of the oil (Greek nu), how thick/sluggish it is (m²/s; given as 32 cSt at 40 °C).
  • f — Darcy friction factor, how much grip the pipe wall exerts on this flow (no units, dimensionless). Found here with the Haaland formula.
  • ε — pipe roughness height (Greek epsilon), the size of tiny bumps inside the wall (m; ≈ 0.045 mm for commercial steel). It appears as ε/D, roughness relative to pipe size.
  • ΔPf — pressure lost to friction along the straight pipe (Pa; shown in MPa). This is the Darcy–Weisbach result.
  • L — length of the supply line (m). Here L = 25 m; it appears as L/D, how many diameters long the pipe is.
  • ρ — density of the hydraulic oil (Greek rho), mass per unit volume (kg/m³). Here ρ = 850 kg/m³. The group ρv²/2 is the flow’s dynamic pressure — the pressure equivalent of its speed.
  • ΔPminor — pressure lost in fittings (valves, bends, manifold) rather than straight pipe (Pa; shown in MPa).
  • K, ΣK — minor-loss coefficient for one fitting (K) and the sum over all fittings (ΣK; Σ is Greek sigma, “add up”) (no units, dimensionless). Here ΣK = 5.

At Q ≈ 185 L/s in the design case, a 0.10 m line runs at ~23 m/s and throws away roughly 1 MPa to friction alone; a 0.15 m line cuts the total loss to a few tenths of a MPa; a 0.20 m line loses almost nothing but holds ~4× the oil volume of the 0.10 m line. The recommendation from this tradeoff is 0.15 m: losses stay under ~2% of working pressure without paying for the largest line. Explore it in the demo — the table there is this exact calculation.

In plain English — Step 3

How wide should the pipe be? Picture drinking through different straws. A skinny straw (0.10 m pipe) forces the oil to blast through at ~23 m/s, rubbing hard against the walls — and about 1 unit of pressure (roughly 5% of the total push) is wasted just on that rubbing. A medium straw (0.15 m) lets the oil cruise at ~10 m/s and wastes almost nothing. A fat straw (0.20 m) wastes even less, but it's pricey and bulky. Verdict: medium wins. The fancy names in the heading are just tools for measuring that rubbing: “Reynolds number” tells us the flow is rough and swirly (turbulent), and “Darcy–Weisbach” is the formula that turns pipe size, length, and speed into a waste number.

Step 4 — Accumulator sizing (polytropic gas expansion)

Derivation. An energy balance over one launch: the accumulators must hand over the train's KE plus everything the path loses, Estored = KE ÷ ηoverall. Dividing by the mean pressure gives a first estimate of the oil volume displaced — but the vessel must also contain the nitrogen, and the gas follows a polytropic path P·Vn = const as it expands from charge pressure (28 MPa) to end pressure (20 MPa). That expansion ratio, not the oil volume alone, fixes the total vessel volume (Theory 5 quantifies how badly the shortcut does).

Estored = KE / ηoverall = 4.59 / 0.70 ≈ 6.6 MJ
Voil ≈ Estored / Pmean ≈ 6.6 MJ / 24 MPa ≈ 273 L of oil displaced
P·Vn = const, n = 1.2 → gas volume grows from V₁ at 28 MPa to V₂ at 20 MPa;
total accumulator volume = V₂ ≈ 1,100 L (exact value in the live demo)
Symbols in Step 4 — where they first appear
  • Estored — energy the accumulators must store and deliver per launch (J; shown in MJ). Here ≈ 6.6 MJ.
  • ηoverall — overall launch efficiency, the share of stored energy that ends up as train KE (no units, a fraction 0–1). Here ηoverall = 0.70.
  • Voil — volume of oil pushed out of the accumulators in one launch (L, litres). Here ≈ 273 L.
  • Pmean — mean (average) accumulator pressure during the launch (Pa; shown in MPa). Here ≈ 24 MPa, midway between 28 MPa charged and 20 MPa spent.
  • P — in this gas law only, pressure of the nitrogen gas (not power) (Pa; shown in MPa). P·Vn = const says pressure × volumen stays the same as the gas expands.
  • V — volume of the nitrogen gas in the accumulator (L; or m³).
  • n — polytropic exponent, describing how the gas expands: n = 1 is slow/isothermal, n = 1.4 is fast/adiabatic (no units, dimensionless). Here n = 1.2.
  • V1 — gas volume when fully charged, at 28 MPa (L).
  • V2 — gas volume after expanding to 20 MPa; the vessel must fit this largest volume, so V2 is the required total accumulator volume (L). Here ≈ 1,100 L.

Comparing n = 1 (isothermal, slow) with n = 1.4 (adiabatic, fast) brackets the answer; n = 1.2 is the realistic middle for a 2.8 s discharge. The simple E ÷ P estimate alone would undersize the vessel, because it ignores that the gas needs room to expand — which is why the polytropic calculation, not the shortcut, is what the demo implements. It is also one of the first things the second-model critique in Section 6 is asked to verify.

In plain English — Step 4

How big a storage tank do we need? Inside each tank, oil pushes against nitrogen gas, squishing it like a spring. During the launch, the gas expands and shoves the oil out. The trap: you can't just divide “energy needed” by “pressure” to get the tank size, because the squished gas needs room to unsquish. Counting that extra room honestly, the tanks need to hold about 1,100 liters total — roughly a hot tub's worth — and they hand over about 273 liters of oil per launch. The “n = 1.2” detail just describes how the gas behaves while it expands in a hurry: not slowly (n = 1), not instantly (n = 1.4), but in between.

Step 5 — Pump recharge

Derivation. Recharge is the Step 4 energy balance run in reverse and slowly: the pump must put Estored back in trecharge seconds, through its own efficiency ηpump. Because trecharge (45 s) is roughly 16× the launch time (2.8 s), the pump power comes out far below the launch peak — this equation is the quantitative form of “store slowly, spend fast.”

Ppump = Estored / (trecharge · ηpump) = 6.6 MJ / (45 s × 0.80) ≈ 182 kW ≈ 244 hp
Symbols in Step 5 — where they first appear
  • Ppump — pump power needed to recharge the accumulators between launches (W; shown in kW, and in hp). Here ≈ 182 kW ≈ 244 hp.
  • trecharge — recharge time allowed for that refill (s, seconds). Here trecharge = 45 s.
  • ηpump — pump efficiency, the share of pump power that ends up stored in the accumulators (no units, a fraction 0–1). Here ηpump = 0.80.
  • Estored — as defined in Step 4: energy to be put back per launch (J; shown in MJ). Here 6.6 MJ.

One 500 hp-class pump covers the design case with margin; two give faster turnaround or redundancy. The headline result of the whole project: the pumps are small — the accumulators do the work.

In plain English — Step 5

How big a pump refills the tank? Surprisingly small. Refilling takes 45 seconds — much longer than the 2.8-second launch — so the pump can take its time. It needs about 182 kW (244 horsepower), and a single pump the size of a sports-car engine (500 hp class) handles it easily. That's the punchline of the whole project: the pump is not the hero. The storage tanks are. Small pump, patient charging, enormous 3-second burst.

Theory 6 · How the page solves it

There is no differential equation to integrate here — the model is algebraic — but it is still solved in a strict sequence, the same sequence the demo code runs on every slider input, and the order matters because each step consumes the previous step's output:

  • 1. From (m, v, t): a, s, F, KE, and the mechanical powers (Step 1).
  • 2. Gross up by ηmotor to peak hydraulic power (Step 2).
  • 3. Set the pressures: end pressure = 0.71 × charge pressure, working pressure ≈ 22 MPa at the design point, mean pressure for the energy step.
  • 4. Qpeak = Phyd,peak ÷ Pworking (Step 2).
  • 5. From Q and D: v = Q/A, Re = vD/ν, then f from the Haaland formula. Haaland is explicit — unlike the Colebrook equation it needs no iteration, which is what makes the live demo instant.
  • 6. ΔPf and ΔPminor from Darcy–Weisbach and ΣK (Step 3); the verdict compares their sum with working pressure.
  • 7. Estored, Voil, the polytropic vessel volume (Step 4), and finally the recharge power (Step 5).

The one numerical compromise is quasi-steady evaluation: instead of time-stepping pressure, flow, and acceleration through the 2.8 s, the model evaluates the balances at snapshots — and sizes everything at the worst snapshot (Theory 7). That is the analogue of the reference example's solver choice: a deliberate trade of transient detail for a transparent, checkable calculation, with the limitation stated rather than hidden.

Theory 7 · Locating the sizing point

The reference example has to locate its hot spot, because the peak temperature almost never sits where you first look. This design has the same trap, in time rather than in space. Two effects stack at the end of the launch: mechanical power F·v is at its maximum because v is at its maximum, and accumulator pressure is at its minimum because the gas has expanded the most. Flow demand Q = P ÷ ΔP therefore peaks at the final moment — numerator largest, denominator smallest. Sizing the flow at mean pressure (24 MPa) instead of the end-of-launch working pressure (22 MPa) would undersize Q by about 9% (≈ 170 L/s instead of 185 L/s), and the line, valves, and motor displacement with it. Every sizing number on this page is therefore evaluated at that located worst point, exactly as a hot-spot temperature is evaluated at its located maximum rather than at a convenient average position.

Theory 8 · Parametric sensitivity

The normalised sensitivity of an output y to an input φ is s(y; φ) = (φ ÷ y) · (∂y ÷ ∂φ) = ∂ ln y ÷ ∂ ln φ — the percentage change in y per 1% change in φ. It is dimensionless, so sensitivities to kilograms, metres, and seconds can be compared on one table. The values below are computed from this page's own model by central differences (±1% perturbations — the finite-difference method, chosen here because the model is algebraic and cheap, unlike the stiff solver in the reference example where finite differences are fragile):

SensitivityValueReading
s(KE; v) — energy vs. target speed+2.00Exact: KE ∝ v². Speed is the most expensive thing to ask for.
s(KE; m) — energy vs. train mass+1.00Exact: KE ∝ m. Mass costs linearly, speed quadratically.
s(Phyd,peak; v)+1.95Nearly v² (thrust itself grows with v through a = v/t); the fixed 5 kN drag dilutes it slightly below 2.
s(Phyd,peak; t)−0.95A 1% longer launch buys ~1% less peak power — but does nothing for total energy.
s(Qpeak; charge pressure)−1.00Higher pressure needs proportionally less flow for the same power — the basic trade a designer can spend either way.
s(ΔPtotal; D) — line loss vs. diameter−4.36The headline sensitivity of the project. Close to the D⁻⁵ friction law; slightly softer than −5 because minor losses scale as D⁻⁴ and f shifts with Re. A 1% larger pipe cuts total line loss by ≈ 4.4%.
s(ΔPtotal; v)+3.74Losses grow almost with v⁴ (flow ∝ v², loss ∝ Q²): asking for more speed punishes an undersized line twice over.
s(Ppump; trecharge)−1.00Exact inverse: double the allowed recharge time, halve the pump.

Check it in the demo: raise the diameter 10% (0.15 → 0.165 m) and the total line loss should fall by about a third (1.1−4.36 ≈ 0.66) — the sensitivity table and the sliders are the same model, so they must agree.

In plain English

“Sensitivity” just asks: if I nudge this one knob by 1%, how much does the answer move? The winner by a mile is pipe diameter: nudge it up 1% and the wasted pressure drops about 4.4%. Speed is the greediest knob — 1% more speed costs 2% more energy. And recharge time is a perfect trade: wait twice as long between launches, need half the pump.

Sensitivity checks worth running in the demo

  • Train mass ±20% — thrust, KE, and accumulator volume all scale ~linearly (s ≈ +1, per the table).
  • Launch time 2.5 s vs. 3.0 s — peak power moves much faster than average power.
  • Undersizing the line to 0.10 m — watch the loss fraction climb from 1.8% to ~11% of working pressure.
  • Oil temperature: colder oil raises ν, lowers Re, raises f — the fixed-ν assumption is a stated limitation.

Section 8 · Reflection

What I learned, and what I want to learn next

In plain English

The three big takeaways, no jargon: (1) Storing energy up and releasing it in a burst beats buying a bigger motor — every time. (2) Pipe size matters way more than you'd guess: make a pipe just a little wider and the wasted energy drops dramatically. (3) When you do your math matters: checking the hardest moment of the launch (the end, when pressure is lowest) is what separates a design that works on paper from one that works on the track. The boxes below say the same things in course language, plus what I'd study next — mainly, tracking the launch second-by-second instead of in snapshots, and using real manufacturers' test data instead of typical values.

What I learned

  • Energy storage decouples average from peak power. The accumulator — not the pump — is the launch. That idea (charge slowly, discharge fast) generalizes to regenerative braking, grid storage, and hydraulic hybrid vehicles.
  • Friction loss is brutally sensitive to diameter (≈1/D⁵ at fixed Q). "Slightly bigger pipe" is often the cheapest power a designer can buy.
  • Where you evaluate a calculation matters: sizing flow at end-of-launch pressure instead of mean pressure is the difference between a design that works on paper and one that works at second 2.7.
  • AI workflow: the plan improved most when it was forced to state its assumptions and name its own weak points in advance — which is exactly how the two-model critique in Section 6 is structured, and why its accept/reject judgment has to stay with the engineer, not the models.

What I want to learn next
