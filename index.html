Sizing a pneumatic launch, from compressor to catch car

How do you size the compressor, receiver tank, piston, and supply line for a compressed-air roller-coaster launch so a train reaches its target speed in the required time — without losing too much pressure to friction along the way? This site is the project: an interactive sizing demo, the full calculation, and the documentation of how it was built with AI.

In plain English

Some roller coasters don't roll down a big hill to get going. Instead, a machine yanks the train forward — really fast — like a slingshot. On the S&S compressed-air coasters (Maxx Force, Dodonpa), the slingshot is a giant blast of squeezed air. This project answers a simple question: how big does that air machine need to be? How hard a push, how much air per launch, how big a storage tank, and how wide a pipe — so the train hits 78 mph in under 3 seconds without wasting pressure along the way. Section 2 · The system

Store slowly, spend fast

A pneumatic launch (S&S's compressed-air design, used on Maxx Force and Dodonpa) separates making power from delivering it. A modest compressor spends ~90 seconds charging a receiver tank; the launch then spends that stored air in under 3 seconds. That is why the peak mechanical power (3.46 MW) can be ~11× the compressor power (305 kW) — the central Transport 1 idea of this project. For contrast, Kingda Ka's Intamin launch did the same store-slowly-spend-fast trick with pressurized oil and hydraulic motors; the S&S design sized here does it with compressed air and a piston.

In plain English

Here's the trick, and it's the whole idea of the project: save up slowly, spend it all at once. A normal compressor isn't strong enough to launch a coaster by itself — that would take a gigantic, expensive machine. So instead, it spends about 90 seconds slowly squeezing air into a storage tank, like inflating a balloon a little at a time. Then — whoosh — the tank releases everything in under 3 seconds. That burst is about 11 times stronger than the compressor alone. It's the same reason you charge your phone for an hour and then it can flash its camera light in a split second: storing energy lets you spend it faster than you made it. In plain English — the 7 steps, simply

1. A compressor slowly squeezes air. 2. A big tank stores it under high pressure, like a coiled spring. 3. Valves snap open. 4. The air races through a fat pipe. 5. It shoves a piston inside a cylinder, hard. 6. The piston drags a small cart down the track. 7. The cart is hooked to the train — and off you go.

Reference cases: Maxx Force (Six Flags Great America, opened 2019) launches 0 → 78 mph (126 km/h, 35 m/s) in 1.8 s, about 1.98 g; Dodonpa (Fuji-Q Highland, S&S Thrust Air 2000) launches 0 → 107 mph (172 km/h, 47.8 m/s) in 1.8 s, about 2.71 g. S&S does not publish piston sizes, tank volumes, or line diameters, so this project sizes its own self-consistent design case (Section 3) — deliberately gentler than both, at 1.27 g — and uses the two real rides only as an order-of-magnitude validation (Section 4). In plain English: the company never published its pipe or tank sizes, so instead of guessing them, this project designs its own slightly gentler launch from scratch — and only uses the real rides as a reality check in Section 4.

Theory 1 · The physical picture

A pneumatic launch is an energy-timing problem before it is a plumbing problem. The train needs 4.59 MJ of kinetic energy delivered in 2.8 s, and — because thrust is roughly constant while speed keeps rising — the instantaneous power at the end of the launch (3.46 MW mechanical) is twice the average (1.73 MW). No reasonably sized compressor delivers that peak directly, so the system separates making power from spending it: the compressor charges a receiver tank slowly, and the compressed air — a gas spring — spends the stored energy in one burst through a piston. Three effects then decide whether the design works. First, the air itself is the working fluid and it is compressible: its density changes with pressure everywhere in the system, so every step needs the ideal-gas law, not a constant density. Second, the air must travel from receiver to cylinder through a real pipe, and the pressure it loses to wall friction and fittings grows brutally as the pipe narrows — roughly with 1/D⁴ to 1/D⁵ — which is why line sizing, a pure Transport 1 question, sits at the centre of the design. Third, expanding air cools: air dropping from 3.0 MPa to cylinder pressure in under 3 seconds falls toward −36 °C, which is a real constraint on valves and seals (Step 6). The “runaway” analogue in this system is not thermal but pneumatic: undersize the line a little and the loss does not grow a little, it explodes — at 0.10 m the air would need Mach 0.70 and the loss would exceed the pressure available to lose.

In plain English

The train needs a lot of energy, all at once, and the hardest moment is the very end of the launch. So the machine cheats on timing: charge slowly, spend fast. Then three things can ruin it — air is squishy, so its density keeps changing and every step has to track that; a pipe that's too skinny wastes pressure, and the waste balloons fast as the pipe shrinks; and expanding air gets seriously cold, which the hardware has to survive. Picking the pipe is the heart of this project.

Theory 2 · The model: control volumes, balances, and assumptions

The model uses three control volumes, and each governing equation in Steps 1–6 is a balance on one of them. The train is treated as a control mass: the linear momentum balance ΣF = m·a on it gives the thrust, and the mechanical-energy balance on it gives the kinetic energy and power the air system must supply (Step 1). The piston and cylinder are a force balance, F = p·A, plus an ideal-gas state calculation, pV = mRT, on the air that fills the swept volume (Steps 2–3). The receiver is a fixed control volume with a mass balance: it can only give up the air between its charged pressure and its end pressure, and that usable pressure drop — not the charge pressure alone — fixes the tank volume (Step 4). The supply line is a fixed control volume between receiver and cylinder: the mass balance on it is continuity in mass-flow form, ṁ = ρ·v·A (Step 5), and the mechanical-energy balance on it is what the pressure is spent on — useful pressure at the piston minus the Darcy–Weisbach friction loss and the minor losses in fittings. Step 6 runs the Step 4 mass balance in reverse, slowly, through a polytropic compression. The model rests on five assumptions, stated up front:

Modeling assumptions

  • Constant acceleration as a first approximation; in reality thrust varies as receiver pressure falls during the launch.
  • Air as an ideal gas at T = 300 K throughout (pV = mRT, R = 287 J/kg·K); at 3.0 MPa this is a good approximation for air, and the second-model critique in Section 6 is asked to check whether a compressibility-factor correction is warranted.
  • In the supply line, density is evaluated at a representative line pressure of 2.0 MPa abs and held constant along the 15 m line — reasonable because the recommended design loses only 6% of line pressure, so density varies little along it.
  • Drag allowance lumped as a constant 5 kN rather than a v² model.
  • Expansion cooling is calculated (Step 6) but its effect on density and flow is not fed back into Steps 3–5 — a stated limitation, priced in Theory 5.

Theory 5 below puts a price on the biggest of these simplifications — the receiver shortcut, the friction-only line, and the isothermal compression bound — so their cost is on the record rather than buried.

Step 1 — Train dynamics (momentum balance on the train)

Derivation. Take the train as the control mass. The linear momentum balance ΣF = m·a, with a taken constant, integrates directly to v = a·t and s = ½·a·t². The piston thrust must supply m·a plus the lumped drag allowance. Force × distance then gives the work the air must do, force × velocity gives the instantaneous mechanical power — which peaks at top speed, because the force is constant while v is still rising — and ½·m·v² is the kinetic energy every later step has to deliver.

a = v / t = 35 / 2.8 = 12.5 m/s² (1.27 g)
s = ½·a·t² = 49 m
F = m·a = 7,500 × 12.5 = 93.8 kN · Fd = F + Fdrag = 93.8 + 5 ≈ 98.8 kN (design thrust)
KE = ½·m·v² = 4.59 MJ · W = Fd·s = 4.84 MJ
Ppeak,mech = Fd·v = 3.46 MW (constant thrust: power peaks at top speed) · Pavg = W / t = 1.73 MW

In plain English — Step 1

How hard do we need to push? The train weighs about as much as five cars (7,500 kg) and has to reach highway speed (78 mph) in 2.8 seconds. That takes a push of roughly 99,000 newtons — imagine about 10 tonnes of weight shoving it forward. Riders feel it as being pressed back into their seats at about 1.3 times normal gravity — noticeably gentler than Maxx Force (about 2 g) or Dodonpa (about 2.7 g). The train needs about 49 meters of track to do it — roughly half a football field — and by the end it's carrying 4.59 million joules of moving energy, delivered in under 3 seconds.

Step 2 — The piston: turning pressure into thrust

Derivation. Pressure is force per unit area by definition, so the force balance on the piston face is simply F = p·A, with A = π·D²/4 for a round piston. Run it backwards: choose a piston diameter, and the cylinder pressure the air must reach is fixed by the design thrust from Step 1. Pressure here is gauge — the piston's back side breathes ordinary atmospheric air, so only the pressure above atmospheric does net work. Add one atmosphere (0.101 MPa) to get the absolute pressure the gas law needs in Step 3.

A = π·D²/4 = π × 0.36² / 4 = 0.1018 m²
pneeded = Fd / A = 98.8 kN / 0.1018 m² = 0.970 MPa gauge (1.07 MPa abs)
Symbols in Step 2 — where they first appear

  • D — piston diameter (m). Here D = 0.36 m — about the size of a large pizza.
In plain English — Step 2

What turns air pressure into a push? A piston — a plug about 36 cm across, like a large pizza — sliding inside a cylinder. Air pressure pushes on the whole face of the plug at once, and the pushes add up: at 0.97 MPa (about 140 psi, roughly four times a car tire's pressure, spread over that whole face), they total the ~99 kN shove the train needs. A wider plug would need less pressure for the same shove — but it would gulp more air every metre, which is the tradeoff Steps 3 and 4 price out.

Step 3 — Air per launch (ideal-gas law)

Derivation. As the piston travels the launch distance s, it sweeps out a volume V = A·s that ends up filled with air at cylinder pressure. The ideal-gas law pV = mRT, solved for density ρ = p ÷ (R·T), turns that volume at that pressure into a mass of air — the amount the receiver must supply per launch (Step 4) and the compressor must replace (Step 6). Differentiating the swept volume gives the flow demand: volumetric flow Q = A·v, which peaks at top speed, and mass flow ṁ = ρ·Q.

V = A·s = 0.1018 × 49 = 4.99 m³ (volume swept by the piston)
ρ = p / (R·T) = 1.07 MPa / (287 × 300) = 12.44 kg/m³ (air density at cylinder pressure)
mair = ρ·V = 12.44 × 4.99 = 62.1 kg per launch
Qpeak = A·v = 3.56 m³/s · ṁpeak = ρ·Qpeak = 44.3 kg/s · ṁavg = mair / t = 22.2 kg/s

  • ṁ — mass flow rate (m-dot) (kg/s). Peak 44.3 kg/s; average 22.2 kg/s.
In plain English — Step 3

How much air does one launch drink? Picture the cylinder as a tube the size of a large pizza, 49 metres long. Filling that whole tube with air squeezed to ten times normal density takes 62 kilograms of air — about the weight of an adult person, thrown at the piston in under 3 seconds. At the hungriest moment, near top speed, air is pouring in at 44 kg every second. We size the pipe (Step 5) and the tank (Step 4) for exactly these numbers.

Step 4 — Receiver sizing (mass balance on the tank)

Derivation. A mass balance on the receiver over one launch: the mass that leaves, mair from Step 3, must equal the drop in the mass stored, and the stored mass follows the ideal-gas law at each pressure. The tank starts at p1 = 3.0 MPa abs and is not emptied to zero — the launch ends at p2 = 1.2 MPa abs, still comfortably above the cylinder's 1.07 MPa abs requirement, so the valves can still meter flow at the end. Only the usable pressure drop (p1 − p2) delivers air, so Vtank = mair·R·T ÷ (p1 − p2). Dividing by p1 alone — the tempting shortcut — would undersize the tank by 40% (Theory 5).

Vtank = mair·R·T / (p₁ − p₂) = 62.1 × 287 × 300 / (3.0 − 1.2) MPa = 2.97 m³ (≈ 3,000 L)
Symbols in Step 4 — where they first appear
  • Vtank — receiver (storage tank) volume (m³; also shown in L). Here ≈ 2.97 m³.
  • p1 — receiver pressure when fully charged (Pa, absolute; shown in MPa). Here p1 = 3.0 MPa abs.
  • p2 — receiver pressure at the end of one launch (Pa, absolute; shown in MPa). Here p2 = 1.2 MPa abs — deliberately kept above the cylinder's 1.07 MPa abs need.

In plain English — Step 4

How big a storage tank do we need? About 3 cubic metres — roughly 3,000 litres, like a small hot tub or a big wardrobe. The subtle part: the tank never empties completely. It starts squeezed to 3.0 MPa and the launch stops drawing at 1.2 MPa, because below that the air couldn't push the piston hard enough anymore. So only the difference between those pressures does the delivering — forget that, and you'd buy a tank 40% too small and the launch would run out of breath. Squeeze the storage harder (4.0 MPa) and the tank shrinks to 1.91 m³, but everything rated for that pressure costs more; 3.0 MPa is the sweet spot.

Step 5 — Supply-line sizing: continuity, Mach, Reynolds, Darcy–Weisbach

Derivation. Apply the two balances to the line as a fixed control volume. For a compressible fluid the mass balance is continuity in mass form, ṁ = ρ·v·A: the mass flow from Step 3 is constant along the line, and at the line's representative density (2.0 MPa abs, ρ = 23.2 kg/m³) it fixes the air velocity for any trial diameter. Two checks follow immediately. The Mach number M = v ÷ c, with c = √(γRT) = 347 m/s, tells whether compressibility inside the line itself matters; and the Reynolds number Re = ρ·v·D ÷ μ, with the friction factor f from the Haaland approximation to the Moody chart, feeds the mechanical-energy balance: friction ΔPf = f·(L/D)·(ρv²/2) plus minor losses ΔPminor = ΣK·(ρv²/2) for the valves, bends, and manifold (ΣK = 6).

ṁ = ρ·v·A · M = v / c · Re = ρ·v·D / μ · 1/√f = −1.8·log₁₀[ (ε/D / 3.7)1.11 + 6.9/Re ] (Haaland)
ΔPf = f · (L/D) · (ρv²/2) · ΔPminor = ΣK · (ρv²/2), ΣK = 6 · L = 15 m
Symbols in Step 5 — where they first appear
  • ṁ — mass flow rate through the line (kg/s). Sized at its peak, 44.3 kg/s.
  • v — here, the speed of the air inside the pipe (not the train) (m/s). Same letter, new meaning — the equation ṁ = ρ·v·A is about the pipe.
  • M — Mach number, air speed ÷ speed of sound (no units, dimensionless). Keep it low; near 1 the flow chokes.
  • c — speed of sound in the line air (m/s). Here c = 347 m/s at 300 K.
  • Re — Reynolds number, a unit-free score for smooth (laminar) vs. swirly (turbulent) flow (no units, dimensionless). Millions here = deeply turbulent.
  • μ — dynamic viscosity of air (Greek mu) (Pa·s). Here μ = 1.85 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s.
  • f — Darcy friction factor, how much grip the pipe wall exerts on this flow (no units, dimensionless). Found here with the Haaland formula; 0.0136 in the recommended line.
  • ε — pipe roughness height (Greek epsilon), the size of tiny bumps inside the wall (m; ≈ 0.045 mm for commercial steel). It appears as ε/D, roughness relative to pipe size.
  • ΔPf — pressure lost to friction along the straight pipe (Pa; shown in kPa). This is the Darcy–Weisbach result.
  • L — length of the supply line (m). Here L = 15 m; it appears as L/D, how many diameters long the pipe is.
  • ΔPminor — pressure lost in fittings (valves, bends, manifold) rather than straight pipe (Pa; shown in kPa).
  • K, ΣK — minor-loss coefficient for one fitting (K) and the sum over all fittings (ΣK; Σ is Greek sigma, “add up”) (no units, dimensionless). Here ΣK = 6.

In plain English — Step 5

How wide should the pipe be? Picture blowing through different straws, except the “straw” has to pass 44 kg of air per second. A skinny straw (0.10 m) forces the air to blast through at 243 m/s — 70% of the speed of sound — and the rubbing and squeezing would waste more pressure than the pipe even has: impossible. A medium straw (0.20 m) wastes 15%. The fat straw (0.25 m) lets the air cruise at 39 m/s and wastes just 6% — and here's the surprise: most of that 6% isn't the pipe walls at all, it's the valves and bends the air has to squeeze through. Verdict: 0.25 m wins. The fancy names are just measuring tools: “Mach number” compares the air's speed to the speed of sound, “Reynolds number” tells us the flow is rough and swirly (turbulent), and “Darcy–Weisbach” turns pipe size, length, and speed into a waste number.

Step 6 — Compressor, recharge, and expansion cooling

Derivation. Recharge is the Step 4 mass balance run in reverse and slowly: the compressor must replace mair = 62.1 kg, raising it from atmospheric pressure to 3.0 MPa abs. For a polytropic compression with exponent n, the work per kilogram is [n ÷ (n−1)]·R·T₁·[(p₂/p₁)(n−1)/n − 1]. With n = 1.3 that is 442 kJ/kg. The same polytropic relation, run as an expansion, also predicts the launch's side effect: air expanding from the receiver's 3.0 MPa down to the cylinder's 1.07 MPa abs cools from 300 K to 237 K (−36 °C) — stated here honestly as a design limitation, because cold air is denser, seals stiffen, and moisture in the air can freeze in the valves.

w = [n/(n−1)]·R·T₁·[(p₂/p₁)(n−1)/n − 1] = 442 kJ/kg (n = 1.3, atmospheric → 3.0 MPa abs)
Ecomp = w · mair = 442 × 62.1 = 27.5 MJ per launch
Pcomp = Ecomp / trecharge = 27.5 MJ / 90 s = 305 kW ≈ 409 hp → one 500 hp compressor suffices
Tafter expansion = 300 K × (1.07 / 3.0)(n−1)/n = 237 K (−36 °C) at n = 1.3 (224 K at n = 1.4 · 253 K at n = 1.2)
Symbols in Step 6 — where they first appear
  • n — polytropic exponent, describing how the gas process sits between isothermal (n = 1, slow, heat exchanged) and adiabatic (n = 1.4, fast, no heat exchange) (no units, dimensionless). Here n = 1.3 for compression.
  • trecharge — recharge time allowed for the refill (s, seconds). Here trecharge = 90 s.
  • η — efficiency (Greek eta) (no units, a fraction 0–1). The 305 kW is the ideal polytropic compression power; a 500 hp (373 kW) installed unit leaves margin above it for motor and drive losses.

The compression model is bracketed, not cherry-picked: a perfectly slow, fully cooled (isothermal, n = 1) compression would need only 18.1 MJ per launch (201 kW over 90 s) — the theoretical floor — while a perfectly fast, uncooled (adiabatic, n = 1.4) compression would need 30.5 MJ (339 kW). The n = 1.3 design value, 27.5 MJ / 305 kW, sits between them, closer to the adiabatic end because 90 seconds is still fast for heat to escape a multi-stage machine. The headline result of the whole project: the compressor is modest — the receiver does the work.

In plain English — Step 6

How big a compressor refills the tank — and what's the catch? Refilling takes 90 seconds — much longer than the 2.8-second launch — so the compressor can take its time. It needs about 305 kW (409 horsepower), and a single compressor the size of a big truck engine (500 hp class) handles it with room to spare. That's the punchline of the whole project: the compressor is not the hero. The storage tank is. The catch is cold: when air expands in a hurry, it chills — here to about −36 °C, colder than a home freezer. Real designs have to dry the air and use valves and seals that still work frozen. This project calculates that cooling honestly but doesn't feed it back into the other steps — that's listed as a limitation, not hidden.

Theory 6 · How the page solves it

There is no differential equation to integrate here — the model is algebraic — but it is still solved in a strict sequence, the same sequence the demo code runs on every slider input, and the order matters because each step consumes the previous step's output:

  • 1. From (m, v, t): a, s, F, Fd, KE, W, and the mechanical powers (Step 1).
  • 2. From Fd and piston D: A = πD²/4 and the required cylinder pressure p = Fd/A, gauge then absolute (Step 2).
  • 3. ρ = p ÷ (R·T) at cylinder pressure; mair = ρ·A·s; ṁpeak = ρ·A·v (Step 3).
  • 4. Vtank = mair·R·T ÷ (p₁ − p₂) (Step 4).
  • 5. From ṁpeak and line D: v = ṁ ÷ (ρline·A), M = v/c, Re = ρvD/μ, then f from the Haaland formula. Haaland is explicit — unlike the Colebrook equation it needs no iteration, which is what makes the live demo instant.
  • 6. ΔPf and ΔPminor from Darcy–Weisbach and ΣK (Step 5); the verdict compares their sum with line pressure.
  • 7. Polytropic compression work per kilogram × mair, divided by trecharge for compressor power; the expansion-cooling estimate from the same polytropic relation (Step 6).

The one numerical compromise is quasi-steady evaluation: instead of time-stepping pressure, flow, and acceleration through the 2.8 s, the model evaluates the balances at snapshots — and sizes everything at the worst snapshot (Theory 7). That is the analogue of the reference example's solver choice: a deliberate trade of transient detail for a transparent, checkable calculation, with the limitation stated rather than hidden.

Theory 7 · Locating the sizing point

The reference example has to locate its hot spot, because the peak temperature almost never sits where you first look. This design has the same trap, in time rather than in space. Two effects stack at the end of the launch: the piston's volumetric demand A·v is at its maximum because v is at its maximum, and the receiver pressure is at its minimum because the tank has given up its air. Mass flow demand ṁ = ρ·A·v therefore peaks at the final moment — so the line, valves, and piston are all sized at peak mass flow, 44.3 kg/s, not at the 22.2 kg/s average; sizing at the average would halve the design flow and the line loss calculation would be optimistic by roughly a factor of four (losses scale with ṁ²). The end of the launch is also where the pressure budget is tightest: the receiver finishes at 1.2 MPa abs against a cylinder requirement of 1.07 MPa abs, leaving only ~0.13 MPa of margin to cover the regulator and the last of the line loss — one more reason the 120 kPa total loss of the recommended line, rather than the 1,016 kPa of the 0.15 m line, is a requirement and not a luxury. Every sizing number on this page is evaluated at that located worst point, exactly as a hot-spot temperature is evaluated at its located maximum rather than at a convenient average position.

Theory 8 · Parametric sensitivity

The normalised sensitivity of an output y to an input φ is s(y; φ) = (φ ÷ y) · (∂y ÷ ∂φ) = ∂ ln y ÷ ∂ ln φ — the percentage change in y per 1% change in φ. It is dimensionless, so sensitivities to kilograms, metres, and seconds can be compared on one table. In plain English

“Sensitivity” just asks: if I nudge this one knob by 1%, how much does the answer move? The winner by a mile is pipe diameter: nudge it up 1% and the wasted pressure drops about 4.1%. Storage pressure is the strongest tank knob (1% more pressure, 1.7% smaller tank). Speed is the greediest knob — 1% more speed costs 2% more energy. And recharge time is a perfect trade: wait twice as long between launches, need half the compressor.

Sensitivity checks worth running in the demo

  • Train mass ±20% — thrust, cylinder pressure, and air per launch all scale ~linearly (s ≈ +1, per the table).
  • Launch time 2.5 s vs. 3.0 s — peak power moves much faster than average power.
  • Undersizing the line to 0.15 m — watch the loss fraction climb from 6% to ~51% of line pressure, and Mach reach 0.31.
  • Piston diameter: a bigger piston trades lower cylinder pressure for more air per launch — watch the receiver volume grow.

Section 8 · Reflection

What I learned, and what I want to learn next

In plain English

The three big takeaways, no jargon: (1) Storing energy up and releasing it in a burst beats buying a bigger motor — every time. (2) Pipe size matters way more than you'd guess: make a pipe just a little wider and the wasted pressure drops dramatically — and in a short fat pipe, the valves and bends waste more than the pipe itself. (3) Air is not just “invisible stuff”: it has weight (62 kg per launch!), it gets freezing cold when it expands, and if you push it through too small a pipe it starts behaving like it's approaching the speed of sound. The boxes below say the same things in course language, plus what I'd study next — mainly, tracking the launch second-by-second instead of in snapshots, and designing for the cold.

What I learned

  • Energy storage decouples average from peak power. The receiver — not the compressor — is the launch. That idea (charge slowly, discharge fast) generalizes to regenerative braking, grid storage, and compressed-air energy storage.
  • Pressure loss is brutally sensitive to diameter (sensitivity ≈ −4.14 here, between the D⁻⁴ minor-loss and D⁻⁵ friction laws). "Slightly bigger pipe" is often the cheapest power a designer can buy — and in a short line, the fittings matter more than the pipe.
  • A compressible working fluid changes every step: density follows pressure through the ideal-gas law, the flow has to be checked against the speed of sound (Mach), and the mass balance — not a volume balance — is what sizes the tank.
  • Where you evaluate a calculation matters: sizing the line at peak mass flow instead of average mass flow is the difference between a design that works on paper and one that works at second 2.7.
  • AI workflow: the plan improved most when it was forced to state its assumptions and name its own weak points in advance — which is exactly how the two-model critique in Section 6 is structured, and why its accept/reject judgment has to stay with the engineer, not the models.

What I want to learn next

  • Transient modeling: time-stepping receiver pressure, temperature, flow, and train acceleration together instead of quasi-steady snapshots.
  • Designing for the cold: air drying, icing in fast valves, and seal materials at −36 °C.
  • Real component data: reading manufacturer compressor maps and valve flow coefficients (Cv) instead of assumed ΣK values.
  • Compressibility corrections: when the ideal-gas law stops being good enough, and how real-gas (Z-factor) sizing changes the receiver and compressor numbers.
  • Why parks choose among launch technologies: compressed air vs. magnetic (LSM/LIM) launches — efficiency, maintenance, and ride feel.
